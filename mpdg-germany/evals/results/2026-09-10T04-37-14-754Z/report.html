## Does the EU Declaration of Conformity need to be in German?

**German market:** confirmed — device is made available in Germany, so MPDG applies
**Answer:** No. The EU Declaration of Conformity may be provided in German **or** English — English alone is compliant. Your distributor is over-applying the rule to the DoC.

MPDG § 8(1) `[verified]`, verbatim: the manufacturer must make the EU Declaration of Conformity under Art. 19(1) MDR available *"in deutscher oder in englischer Sprache"*. That is an explicit either/or, and it is the one document Germany deliberately does not require in German.

Your distributor is right about the general direction, though, just not about this document. The rule is asymmetric:

| Item | MDR requires | Germany adds | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| EU Declaration of Conformity | not carried here | German **or English** — no translation duty | MPDG § 8(1) `[verified]` |
| Information for users and patients (IFU, labelling) | not carried here | **German**, and it is a condition of supply — the device may only be handed over to users/patients if this is met | MPDG § 8(2) sentence 1 `[verified]` |
| Same, where users are exclusively professionals | not carried here | English or another easily understood language permitted, but only *in begründeten Fällen* **and** exclusively professional users **and** safety-related information still in German or the user's language — all three, cumulatively | MPDG § 8(2) sentence 2 `[verified]` |
| Implant information under Art. 18(1) MDR | not carried here | **German**. No professional-user exception reaches it | MPDG § 8(3) `[verified]` |

So: keep the English DoC, and check instead whether your IFU and user-facing information are in German. That is where a Class IIa device sold to lay users actually gets caught, and § 8(2) makes it a precondition for handing the product over, not just a paperwork defect.

One adjacent point worth having on file: if you ever take a field safety corrective action in Germany, the Field Safety Notice must be written in German — MPDG § 73(1) `[verified]`. That attaches to the action being taken in Germany, not to where you are established, so it reaches you as a Dutch manufacturer. The § 73(2) duties to document the FSCA, review its effectiveness, and notify completion apply to manufacturers, authorised representatives and importers *established in Germany*, so on the facts as you have given them they fall on your German-established importer rather than on you `[verified]`.

**What would change this:** nothing about the DoC — the either/or in § 8(1) does not turn on class, on where the manufacturer sits, or on who the user is. What does change is the § 8(2) analysis: whether your device goes to lay users or exclusively to professionals, and whether it is implantable (§ 8(3)).

## Short answer

**Yes for the DoC — but no, not "everything."** Your distributor is right about the Declaration of Conformity and overreaching on the rest.

## The DoC specifically

MDR (EU) 2017/745, **Article 19(1)**, last sentence:

> "It shall contain, as a minimum, the information set out in Annex IV and **shall be translated into an official Union language or languages required by the Member State(s) in which the device is made available**."

So the DoC language isn't your choice — it's set by each destination Member State. Germany requires German for medical device information under the MPDG (Medizinprodukterecht-Durchführungsgesetz), so a German DoC is the defensible position. English-only for the German market is not.

Two practical notes:

- **Make it bilingual, one document.** A two-column EN/DE DoC keeps a single version-controlled artifact. The most common audit finding here isn't a missing translation — it's a German DoC that's a revision behind the English one after a scope or standards change. Article 19(1) also requires you to *continuously update* the DoC, and that obligation applies to every language version.
- **You own the translation, not the distributor.** Article 16(2) lets a distributor translate the *information supplied with the device* (IFU, labelling) without becoming a manufacturer — but only if they meet the Article 16(3)/(4) conditions (QMS covering the translation, 28 days' advance notice to you and the competent authority). The DoC isn't "information supplied with the device" at all, so that carve-out doesn't apply. Keep the German DoC as your document, issued and signed by you.

## Where your distributor is overreaching

| Document | German required? |
|---|---|
| Label, IFU, packaging | **Yes** — Annex I §23.1(f) leaves language to the Member State; Germany requires German |
| EU Declaration of Conformity | **Yes** — Art. 19(1) |
| Technical documentation (Annexes II/III) | **No** — English is standard and agreed with your notified body |
| Clinical evaluation report, risk file, PMS/PSUR | **No** — these are tech doc |
| SSCP | **N/A** — Art. 32 only applies to implantable and Class III |

The one caveat on tech doc: **Article 10(14)** says that on a reasoned request from a competent authority, you must provide the documentation demonstrating conformity "in an official Union language determined by the Member State concerned." So BfArM or the ZLG *could* ask for German. That's a reactive, on-request obligation — it is not a reason to pre-translate your technical file, and no one should let a distributor turn it into one.

Worth checking whether the distributor's demand is actually a regulatory reading or a commercial/liability-comfort ask dressed up as one. Under Article 14(2)(a) their verification duty is just that a DoC *has been drawn up* — they aren't the arbiter of your documentation set.

## What I'd do

1. Issue a bilingual EN/DE DoC now — it's a one-page Annex IV document, cheaper to translate than to argue about.
2. Add the German version to your change-control so it re-issues whenever the English does.
3. Confirm your German IFU and labelling are complete (that's the requirement with real enforcement exposure).
4. Push back in writing on the technical documentation, citing Art. 10(14) as request-based.

**One thing to verify:** the exact MPDG provision setting German as the required language — I believe it's the §8 *Sprachenregelungen* area, but I couldn't confirm the section number or its precise scope for the DoC without web access. Have your RA lead or German regulatory counsel check the current MPDG text and the Commission's Member State language-requirements overview. Note the MPDG has a narrow allowance for English IFUs for professional-user-only devices — if your Class IIa device is professional-use-only, that may be worth checking too, though it wouldn't extend to the DoC.

Short answer: not because "everything must be translated" — that's too broad — but Germany is one of the markets where you should just do it. Here's the actual legal chain.

## What the MDR says

Article 19(1) MDR, last sentence: the EU declaration of conformity "shall be translated into an official Union language or languages **required by the Member State(s) in which the device is made available on the market**."

So the MDR itself doesn't mandate translation. It delegates the question to each Member State. That means the answer is per-market, and for Germany it lives in the **MPDG** (Medizinprodukterecht-Durchführungsgesetz), in its language provision (Sprachenanforderungen — around § 8; verify the current section numbering, I can't browse from this session to confirm the cite).

My understanding of the German position is that the explicit German-language mandate targets the Annex I Section 23 information — label, IFU, safety information — and that German authorities accept an English DoC. But that is exactly the point you should have someone confirm against the current MPDG text or ask your Benannte Stelle / the competent Land authority, rather than take from me, because it's a national-law detail and the consequence of being wrong is a market-access argument with your distributor.

## Where your distributor is wrong, and where they have a point

Wrong: "everything has to be translated." Technical documentation, your QMS, your clinical evaluation, your PMS plan — none of that goes into German for the German market. Authorities can request documentation and will generally accept English (and can require German if they insist, but that's on request, not a placing-on-the-market condition).

Right in substance, though: under Article 14(2)(b) a distributor must **verify** that the EU DoC has been drawn up before making the device available. A German distributor who can't read the document has a plausible reason to want one they can. Add German public procurement and hospital tenders, which routinely demand German-language documentation regardless of what the regulation requires, and you'll meet this request repeatedly.

## What I'd actually do

Translate it. Annex IV is roughly ten data fields on one page — manufacturer name and SRN, Basic UDI-DI, device description, risk class, conformity assessment route, notified body name and NB number, the standards applied, place and date, signatory. The translation cost is trivial against the friction of arguing about it in every tender.

Practical handling:
- Keep **English as the master version** and mark the German one "Übersetzung der Originalfassung" / translation of the original.
- Control it as a QMS document (ISO 13485 § 4.2.4). Article 19(1) requires the DoC to be **continuously updated** — every update to the master must trigger the translation, or you'll ship a German DoC citing a superseded NB certificate.
- Bilingual single-document format (EN/DE in parallel columns) is common and avoids version drift between two files entirely. I'd recommend that over separate files.

## What genuinely must be in German

Don't let the DoC question distract from the things that are hard requirements: label, IFU, and safety information under Annex I Section 23, plus field safety notices to German users, and correspondence with BfArM. Note that Germany permits English IFU for some devices supplied exclusively to professional users under conditions — but if your Class IIa device reaches lay users at all, that door is closed, and most manufacturers translate anyway.

Finally: run this same question for your other markets rather than settling it once. Several Member States do require a national-language DoC, and the set isn't the same as the set requiring national-language IFU. A small per-market language matrix — DoC / label / IFU / FSN columns — is the artifact you want, and your distributors are the cheapest source for filling it in.

## Can the IFU for a lab-only benchtop analyser be English-only in Germany?

**German market:** confirmed — sold to hospital laboratories in Germany
**Answer:** No, not English-*only*. You can put the bulk of the IFU in English under the § 8(2) sentence 2 exception, but the safety-related information must still be provided in German (or the user's language), and you must be able to justify the case.

| Item | MDR/IVDR requires | Germany adds | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Instructions for use (information for users) | not carried here | German by default — and it is a **condition of supply**: products may only be handed over if German information is provided | MPDG § 8(2) sentence 1 `[verified]` |
| Same, where users are exclusively professionals | not carried here | English (or another easily understood language) permitted, but only **in begründeten Fällen** | MPDG § 8(2) sentence 2 `[verified]` |
| Safety-related information within that IFU | not carried here | **German** (or the user's language) even when the exception applies | MPDG § 8(2) sentence 2, final clause `[verified]` |
| EU Declaration of Conformity | not carried here | German **or English** — English alone is fine, no translation needed | MPDG § 8(1) `[verified]` |

The exception is cumulative — three conditions, all required:

1. **Justified case** (*begründeter Fall*) — MPDG does not define this, and I can't tell you how BfArM reads it. "Our users read English" is the argument you'd have to make and document; it is not automatic.
2. **Exclusively professional users** — your facts look clean here: hospital labs only, never patients, never home users. Note this is about who the *information* is intended for, so keep it that way in your IFU, labelling and marketing; a patient-facing or self-test variant would break it.
3. **Safety-related information also in German** — this is the one that defeats "English only". Warnings, cautions, contraindications, biological/electrical/mechanical hazard information, disposal of infectious material, and the like need German alongside. MPDG doesn't enumerate what counts as *sicherheitsbezogen*, so scope that conservatively with your regulatory function.

Practically: a German IFU is the low-risk route. If you want the English-language IFU, the shippable shape is English IFU + German safety content (either bilingual in the safety sections or a German safety supplement in the box), plus a documented justification in your technical file.

On the competitor: what another manufacturer ships is not a legal basis, and you can't see their justification file or whether they carry German safety content you haven't noticed. Don't build your position on it.

**What would change this:** any patient- or lay-user route to the device (self-test, near-patient use outside a lab, patient-facing outputs) removes the exception entirely and the whole IFU must be German. Conversely, nothing here obliges you to translate the DoC.

## Can we ship the IFU in English only? (benchtop IVD analyser, German hospital labs)

**German market:** confirmed — sold to hospital laboratories in Germany, so MPDG applies.
**Answer:** Almost, but not as stated — English-only is available to you under the professional-user exception, *except* that the safety-related information must still be provided in German (or the user's language). So the IFU is a mixed-language document, not an English-only one.

### The rule, § 8(2)

Sentence 1 is the default: products may only be handed over to users and patients in Germany if the information intended for them is provided **in German**. That is a condition of supply, not a labelling nicety.

Sentence 2 gives your exception, and it is **cumulative** — all three must hold:

| Condition | Your situation |
|---|---|
| A justified case (*begründeter Fall*) | You need to be able to state one; "all our users are lab professionals" is the shape of the argument, but it is your file to build. |
| Information intended **exclusively** for professional users | Holds, on your facts — hospital labs only, never patients or home users. |
| **Safety-related information also in German** or the user's language | This is the one that defeats "English only". |

### MDR/IVDR vs. the German delta

| Item | MDR/IVDR requires | Germany adds | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Instructions for use, professional users | Not carried here | German by default; English permitted only in a justified case, for exclusively professional users, **and** with safety-related information still in German | MPDG § 8(2) `[verified]` |
| Safety-related information in the IFU | Not carried here | German (or the user's language) regardless of the professional-user exception | MPDG § 8(2) sentence 2, final clause `[verified]` |
| EU Declaration of Conformity | Not carried here | German **or** English — English alone is fine; translating it is over-application | MPDG § 8(1), which names IVDR Art. 17(1) explicitly `[verified]` |

**Practical shape:** an English IFU with the safety-related content — warnings, precautions, contraindications, biohazard and handling cautions, anything whose misreading creates risk — rendered in German as well. Where the line falls between "safety-related" and merely instructional is a judgment call the Act does not draw for you, and it is the part worth having a regulatory professional sign off on. Erring wide costs translation; erring narrow puts the supply condition in § 8(2) sentence 1 in play.

**What would change this:** if any of the information is intended for anyone other than professional users, or reaches patients, the exception falls away entirely and sentence 1's German requirement applies to all of it.

On the competitor: what another manufacturer does is not a legal basis, and you cannot see their file. They may have a justified case documented and German safety sections you have not noticed, or they may be non-compliant and unchallenged. Either way it does not transfer.

Graders — what "good" means for this case
criteriallm

MDR Art. 89(8) governs FSNs but does not impose German. MPDG § 73(1) does: where a manufacturer takes an FSCA im Geltungsbereich dieses Gesetzes, the Field Safety Notice must be written in German. A response working from MDR alone misses this entirely.

PASS requires: (1) It flags that the FSN needs to be in German for the German market, citing MPDG § 73(1) or clearly attributing it to German national law. (2) It does not claim the English FSN is fine everywhere.

Bonus, not required: noting § 73(2)'s completion-notification duty.

Note for the judge: § 73(2) sentence 3 turns on the authorised representative sitting outside the Geltungsbereich — that is, outside Germany. Ireland is outside Germany, so the condition IS satisfied. The prompt names no German-established importer, so the duty is moot on these facts. Do not penalise a response that omits the point; do not reward one that states the condition is unmet.

FAIL if the answer is "no problem" or if it addresses only MDR Art. 89 without reaching the German language requirement.
