## Bottom line: Class IIa, MDR Annex VIII Rule 11, first indent

Plan for a notified body. I'd advise against the Class I route, even though your framing points at it.

## Why "it's the treatment, not information" doesn't land you in Class I

Rule 11's final indent ("all other software is classified as class I") is a residual catch-all. To reach it you have to get past two gates: the software must *not* provide information used to take decisions with diagnostic or therapeutic purposes, and must *not* monitor physiological processes.

A CBT-I programme fails the first gate. Sleep restriction scheduling outputs a prescribed sleep window; stimulus control outputs behavioural instructions; cognitive restructuring outputs tailored content. All of it is information the patient then acts on — when to go to bed, when to get up, whether to leave the bedroom, whether to continue the programme. Those are therapeutic decisions.

Two points on your specific reasoning:

- **"No clinician in the loop" cuts against you, not for you.** Nothing in Rule 11 confines "decisions" to healthcare professionals, and MDCG 2019-11 treats the decision-taker as whoever it is — HCP, patient, or lay user. Removing the clinician doesn't remove you from Rule 11; it removes a mitigating filter, which is a risk-file problem, not a classification escape.
- **"The treatment itself" isn't a recognised category.** Rule 11 has no third door for "software that *is* the therapy." The genuine Class I space under the last indent is things like e-diaries and simple data capture, storage/transmission/display without interpretation, communication tools, and devices that qualified for reasons unrelated to therapeutic output. A device whose intended purpose is literally *"to treat chronic insomnia in adults"* is not that. Self-declaring Class I on an explicit treatment claim, prescription-only, is the kind of position that fails at competent-authority review or at reimbursement (BfArM/DiGA, and equivalents) even if it gets to market.

## Is IIb in play?

Rule 11 escalates to IIb where the decisions may cause serious deterioration of health or a surgical intervention. Worth taking seriously, because sleep restriction is not benign: acute sleep curtailment causes daytime sleepiness (driving and occupational accidents), can precipitate mania in undiagnosed bipolar disorder, lowers seizure threshold in epilepsy, and an unscreened patient may have OSA that the programme masks or worsens.

IIa is defensible and is the market norm — but only if your risk file actually shows the controls: contraindication screening at onboarding, exclusion/escalation pathways, explicit sleepiness and driving warnings, a floor on how far the sleep window compresses, and safety-netting for non-response or deterioration. Without those, expect a notified body to push for IIb. Class III (death or irreversible deterioration) isn't credible here.

## Rules checked and excluded

- **Implementing rule 3.3 (Annex VIII):** software independent of any other device is classified in its own right — confirms your standalone analysis, no inheritance from a parent device.
- **Rule 9 / Rule 10:** no administration or exchange of energy, no ionising radiation.
- **Rule 22:** no integrated diagnostic function driving automated therapy delivery (closed loop).
- **Rules 1–8:** invasive/non-invasive rules, not applicable to standalone software.
- **Implementing rule 3.5:** where several rules apply, the strictest governs. Only Rule 11 applies, so no interaction.

## What IIa commits you to

Notified body conformity assessment (Annex IX Ch. I + III, or Annex XI), ISO 13485 QMS, clinical evaluation under Article 61 with a PMCF plan — and for a novel therapeutic claim, expect the NB to want your own clinical investigation rather than equivalence or literature alone. Plus IEC 62304 (likely software safety class B given the sleepiness/accident pathway, so justify it explicitly), ISO 14971, IEC 62366-1, MDCG 2019-16 cybersecurity, UDI, and GSPR Annex I — note that GSPR 22 lay-user requirements still apply even though the product is prescription-only.

Document the classification rationale in the technical file, including why Rule 11 first indent rather than the last indent. Reviewers want to see you engaged with the question rather than defaulted.

## What would change this

- Adding actigraphy or wearable-derived sleep tracking engages Rule 11's second indent (monitoring physiological processes) — still IIa, and only IIb if it monitored vital parameters whose variation could cause immediate danger, which sleep staging doesn't.
- Adding screening or triage (e.g. an OSA or depression screen that returns a result to the patient) is a separate intended purpose needing its own Rule 11 analysis.
- Adding a clinician dashboard that informs treatment decisions stays IIa but changes your intended purpose statement and intended user groups.

## Bottom line: **Class IIa**, under Annex VIII Rule 11, first limb

I'd plan and budget for IIa (Notified Body, Annex IX Chapter I+III or Annex XI). The Class I argument your framing points at is real and well-known, but it's a minority reading and I wouldn't build a regulatory strategy on it.

## Walking Rule 11

Rule 11 has three limbs:

1. **"Software intended to provide information which is used to take decisions with diagnosis or therapeutic purposes"** → IIa, escalating to IIb (serious deterioration / surgical intervention) or III (death / irreversible deterioration).
2. **Software intended to monitor physiological processes** → IIa, or IIb for vital parameters where variation could cause immediate danger.
3. **All other software** → Class I.

Limb 2 doesn't apply — sleep diaries and adherence tracking aren't monitoring of physiological processes in the Rule 11 sense (self-reported data, no physiological measurement, no clinical monitoring purpose). If you later add wearable-derived sleep staging or actigraphy used for a monitoring claim, revisit this.

So the question is entirely limb 1 vs limb 3.

## Why limb 1 catches you despite "no clinician in the loop"

Your argument is the classic **Rule 11 therapy gap**: the rule is drafted around *information-provider* software, and software that *is* the intervention arguably provides information to no decision-maker at all, so it falls through to "all other software" = Class I.

The problem is that "decisions" in Rule 11 is not limited to clinician decisions. MDCG 2019-11 treats the decision-taker as the *user* — healthcare professional **or** patient/lay person. And your product does, concretely, output information the patient acts on:

- a prescribed sleep window ("in bed 00:30–05:30, no naps")
- stimulus-control instructions ("out of bed after 20 minutes awake")
- week-by-week titration of that window based on their diary data
- cognitive restructuring prompts targeting sleep-related beliefs

That is patient-facing information, individualised to that patient's data, driving therapeutic behaviour. A Notified Body will read that as limb 1 without much hesitation. "The software is the therapy" is a description of the business model, not an exemption — the mechanism by which it delivers therapy is instructing the patient.

The "no clinician in the loop" point actually cuts *against* you rather than for you: it removes the human filter that would otherwise sit between the software's output and the patient's action, which is a risk-increasing feature, not a declassifying one.

**Where the Class I argument survives:** if the programme were fixed, non-adaptive psychoeducational content — the same six modules for everyone, no personalisation from patient input — the "provides information used to take decisions" characterisation gets much weaker and Class I becomes defensible. Sleep restriction scheduling that recomputes from the patient's diary is the opposite of that. Your algorithmic titration is the single feature that most clearly puts you in IIa.

## Why not IIb

Escalation to IIb requires that the decisions driven by your information "may cause a serious deterioration of a person's state of health." The recognised harms of CBT-I are real but are normally judged as manageable and not "serious deterioration" in the Rule 11 sense:

- **Sleep restriction causes transient daytime sleepiness**, with a genuine road-traffic and occupational-machinery risk in weeks 2–3
- **Unmasking or precipitating mania** in undiagnosed bipolar patients
- **Masking untreated OSA or another organic sleep disorder** by relabelling it as behavioural insomnia and delaying diagnosis
- Insomnia's comorbidity with depression and suicidality

I'd expect IIa to hold, but treat it as contingent, not settled. The things that keep you at IIa rather than pushing you to IIb are contraindication screening at onboarding (bipolar disorder, untreated OSA / high STOP-BANG, seizure disorder, professional drivers and shift workers, pregnancy), explicit driving/machinery warnings tied to the sleep-restriction phase, floor limits on how short the prescribed window can go (don't titrate below ~5 hours), and escalation rules that push the patient out to a clinician on worsening or on a suicidality flag. If your risk analysis under ISO 14971 can't reduce the driving-accident pathway to acceptable, or if you *don't* screen for bipolar and OSA, a Notified Body has a credible route to IIb. Get those controls into the design and the IFU, not just into your risk file.

Class III is not in play — no plausible path to death or irreversible deterioration as the *direct* impact of a sleep-scheduling decision.

## Other rules to check

Annex VIII implementing rule 3.5: if multiple rules apply, the highest class wins. Rules 9, 10, 15, 20, 22 don't bite for standalone CBT-I software — no energy administration, no diagnosis/monitoring claim, no closed-loop therapeutic-with-integrated-diagnostic function. Rule 11 is your rule.

## Two things that would change the answer

- **Any diagnostic or screening claim.** If onboarding "identifies" chronic insomnia, or flags likely OSA, you've added a diagnostic purpose to your intended purpose and the analysis reruns — potentially with a worse escalation profile than the therapeutic limb.
- **Intended purpose wording.** "To treat chronic insomnia in adults by delivering CBT-I" is clean and I'd keep it. Resist adding "as an alternative to pharmacotherapy" or comorbidity claims (insomnia in depression, in cancer) — each widens the population and strengthens the escalation argument.

## Practical consequence

IIa means a Notified Body, a full QMS to ISO 13485, clinical evaluation with your own clinical investigation (literature on CBT-I generally won't establish equivalence to *your* implementation — MDR Article 61(3) equivalence for software is hard), IEC 62304 software lifecycle at Class B or C depending on your safety classification, IEC 62366 usability, and PMCF. Plan 12–18 months from a standing start. If you were assuming Class I self-declaration, that assumption is the thing to correct first — it changes your timeline and headcount more than anything else in this analysis.

Worth noting that Rule 11 is widely criticised for exactly the ambiguity you've spotted and has been a target of MDR reform discussion; nothing has changed the law as far as I know, so classify against the text as it stands.

This is my read of the rule, not formal regulatory advice — worth a short pre-submission conversation with your intended Notified Body, since IIa vs I is precisely the question they'll want to have settled before you file.

## Class I — Rule 11, third indent (MDR Annex VIII)

**Reasoning chain:**

Rule 11 is the software rule and it has three limbs:

1. *Information used to take decisions with diagnostic or therapeutic purposes* → IIa (or IIb/III depending on harm severity)
2. *Monitoring physiological processes* → IIa (IIb if vital parameters with immediate danger)
3. *All other software* → **Class I**

Your device sits in limb 3. As you've described it, it doesn't produce an output that a human then acts on to make a therapeutic decision — the software's output **is** the therapy. There's no clinician receiving information, and no monitoring of physiological processes (self-reported sleep diaries aren't physiological monitoring in the sense of Rule 11's second limb). Nothing in Rules 9, 12, 13, 15, 21 or 22 catches it either: no energy administration or exchange, no medicinal substance, no closed-loop diagnostic function determining patient management. So Rule 3.3 (strictest-rule-applies) leaves you at Class I.

Note that "prescription" status is regulatorily irrelevant under MDR — unlike the US, there's no Rx/OTC axis in classification.

## The exposure you should plan around

The Class I conclusion is correct but it is not comfortable, and I'd want you to go in with your eyes open:

**Sleep restriction scheduling is the weak point.** If the programme takes patient-entered sleep diary data and computes an individualised time-in-bed window that it presents to the patient, a competent authority can argue that this *is* information used to take a therapeutic decision — with the patient as the decision-maker. MDCG 2019-11 is explicit that the decision-maker under Rule 11 need not be a healthcare professional; lay users and patients count. That reading puts you at **IIa**. Harm severity wouldn't push you to IIb or III on the standard analysis, so IIa is the realistic worst case.

Whether you land in I or IIa turns substantially on how the sleep restriction module is architected and described: a fixed protocol the patient follows reads as treatment delivery; a computed, personalised prescription rendered back to the patient reads as decision-support output. Rule 3.5 also means modules are assessed individually and the device takes the highest resulting class — so this one module can pull the whole product to IIa on its own.

**A secondary risk argument exists** and I'd address it pre-emptively in your risk file rather than wait to be asked: sleep restriction carries real hazards — daytime vigilance impairment (driving), mania precipitation in undiagnosed bipolar disorder, seizure threshold effects. Your mitigation is screening and contraindications, but an authority that finds those inadequate has a route to argue serious deterioration of health. Documented contraindication screening at onboarding closes this off.

## What I'd actually recommend

Class I is defensible and it's where a strict reading of Rule 11 lands you. But if you're targeting Germany's DiGA pathway or want to avoid a national authority challenging your self-declaration post-launch, the pragmatic question is whether the ~12–18 months and notified body cost of IIa buys you enough certainty and market credibility to be worth it. Several EU DTx have shipped as Class I; several comparable ones went IIa deliberately. That's a commercial call, not a regulatory one, and it's yours.
