## Class III

Under EU MDR 2017/745, the governing text isn't Rule 11 — it's the implementing rule in **Annex VIII, Section 3.3**:

> "Software, which drives a device or influences the use of a device, shall fall within the same class as the device. If the software is independent of any other device, it shall be classified in its own right."

Your app drives the pump (it sets the delivery rate) and is not independent of it. So it inherits the pump's class: **III**.

### Why the "it doesn't diagnose anything" reasoning doesn't help

Rule 11 is the classification rule for *independent* MDSW — software that provides information used for diagnostic or therapeutic decisions. That's the rule people reach for, and it's the wrong one here. Yours isn't information-providing software that a clinician reads and acts on; it's the control interface that actuates the pump. Section 3.3 is applied before you ever get to Rule 11, and 3.3 doesn't care about diagnosis. Nor does "under clinician control" reduce the class — the clinician setting a value in your UI is exactly the "drives the device" scenario, not an exemption from it. MDCG 2019-11 (qualification and classification of MDSW) is consistent on both points.

### The one question that actually changes your answer

Not the class — that's III either way — but the regulatory *route*:

- **Part of the pump** (placed on the market as a component of the device, same technical documentation, same certificate, same UDI-DI family): most likely, if the app is the only way to set the rate. The pump's existing Class III certificate covers it; you're managing a change to an existing device, not certifying a new one.
- **Accessory** (separately CE-marked, e.g. sold standalone, or works across a product line you didn't originally certify together): accessories are classified in their own right (Annex VIII 3.2), but 3.3 still lands you on Class III — now with its own technical documentation, own Annex IX assessment, own UDI.

Worth settling explicitly with your notified body, because the second path is substantially more work than most teams expect.

### Practical consequences of Class III

- Annex IX conformity assessment: full QMS audit **plus** technical documentation assessment of the specific device by the notified body (no self-certification, no sampling relief).
- **IEC 62304 software safety class C** — an incorrect or unintended infusion rate can cause death or serious injury. Full architectural design, detailed design, unit verification, and SOUP documentation for the tablet OS and every third-party library.
- Clinical evaluation with data (Article 61 — Class III implantables aside, expectations are high), plus PMCF.
- Article 54 scrutiny doesn't apply (that's Class III implantables), but expect heavy scrutiny anyway.

### Things I'd flag now, before the class debate is even resolved

- **General-purpose tablet as the platform.** You need to bound the computing platform in your technical documentation — OS versions, hardware, what happens on OS auto-update, what happens if another app steals focus or the battery dies mid-titration. This is usually the hardest part of a tablet-based pump controller.
- **Cybersecurity** — MDCG 2019-16, and the interface between tablet and pump is now a safety-critical channel.
- **IEC 62366-1 usability engineering** — for a Class III rate-setting UI, a summative validation study is effectively mandatory.
- **Alarms** — if the app displays or relays any alarm condition, IEC 60601-1-8 comes into scope, and alarm reliability on a consumer tablet is a genuinely hard problem.
- **Risk control allocation.** If the pump itself enforces hard rate limits independently of the app, say so prominently in your risk file; it doesn't lower the class, but it materially changes the risk argument.

I'm giving you the reasoning from the regulation text, not a regulatory sign-off — get your RA lead or notified body to confirm the part-of-device vs. accessory call in writing, since that one determines your submission structure.

## Short answer: Class III

Not via Rule 11 — via the implementing rule.

**MDR Annex VIII, Chapter II, Rule 3.3:**
> "Software, which drives a device or influences the use of a device, shall fall within the same class as the device. If the software is independent of any other device, it shall be classified in its own right."

Your app sets the infusion rate. That is *driving* the pump in the most literal sense — it commands the therapy-delivering actuator. So it inherits the pump's class: **III**.

Rule 11 doesn't apply here. Rule 11 is for software that's independent of another device and provides information for diagnostic/therapeutic decisions. Yours isn't providing information for a clinician to act on; it *is* the control path. (Even if you forced Rule 11, "could cause death or irreversible deterioration" would land you at III anyway — so there's no cheaper reading.)

The "it doesn't diagnose anything" argument doesn't help. Diagnosis is only one route to a high class; control of a hazardous therapy is another.

## The question that actually matters more

**Is this a separate device, or part of the pump?** This has bigger practical consequences than the class, and MDCG 2019-11 is the guidance to read.

- **Part of the pump** (same manufacturer, placed on the market as one system, covered by the pump's technical documentation, clinical evaluation, and CE certificate): no separate CE mark, no separate UDI-DI for the app, no second conformity assessment. Given it's *your* pump, this is usually the right and much lighter path.
- **Accessory placed on the market separately**: classified in its own right (Art. 51(1)) — still Class III by Rule 3.3 — with its own tech doc, UDI, declaration of conformity, and notified body route.

If the app ships only with your pump and is versioned with it, argue for "part of the device." If you sell/distribute it independently (app store, works across pump models, separate release cadence), a notified body will likely push you to accessory.

## Consequences either way

- **Conformity assessment**: Annex IX (QMS + technical documentation assessment) — notified body involved, no self-certification. Art. 54 clinical evaluation consultation targets Class III *implantable* devices, so it shouldn't catch you, but confirm with your NB.
- **IEC 62304 safety class C.** Separate axis from MDR class, but a wrong rate on a Class III pump means death or serious injury, and no external risk control fully mitigates it from the software's side. Full architecture, detailed design, unit verification, and SOUP requirements apply.
- **The tablet is part of your risk story.** General-purpose OS, other apps, screen failures, touch misregistration, backgrounding, battery death mid-titration, OS auto-updates. You must specify minimum host requirements and treat the platform as SOUP-adjacent. IEC 62366-1 usability engineering will be heavily scrutinized on rate entry — decimal point errors, unit confusion, confirmation steps.
- **Displaying the current rate is a safety function**, not a convenience. If the app shows a stale or wrong rate, the clinician acts on it. Treat display integrity, staleness detection, and connection-loss behavior as risk controls with real requirements.
- **Pump-side independent limits.** Your best architectural lever: the pump enforces its own hard rate bounds and validates commands, so the app can't command an unsafe rate even if it misbehaves. Doesn't lower the MDR class, but it substantially improves the risk file and the 62304 argument.

## Why limb 1 doesn't apply

- the diagnostic information is authored and signed by the radiologist *before* the software touches it;
- the software's action is presentational and logistical — re-laying-out and delivering an unchanged clinical payload;
- it contributes no new clinical content, no derived value, no prioritisation, no interpretation. Remove the software and the GP gets the identical clinical information by another route.

- selecting, summarising, truncating, or reordering content by clinical significance;
