## Short answer: Class III

Your app inherits the pump's class. That comes from MDR Annex VIII, Chapter II, **implementing rule 3.3**:

> "Software, which drives a device or influences the use of a device, shall fall within the same class as the device. If the software is independent of any other device, it shall be classified in its own right."

Setting and adjusting the delivery rate is the textbook case of *driving* a device — it's not merely "influencing" it. So the pump is Class III → the app is Class III.

## Why "it doesn't diagnose" doesn't get you to Class I

That reasoning is a Rule 11 argument, and **Rule 11 doesn't apply here**. Rule 11 governs software that is independent of another device — MDSW that provides information for diagnostic or therapeutic decisions, or monitors physiological processes. Per MDCG 2019-11, driving/influencing software is classified under rule 3.3 instead, and you never reach Rule 11's "all other software is Class I" catch-all.

Two related traps in your framing:

- **"Under clinician control" is not a de-risking factor.** A human in the loop doesn't lower the class when the software is the thing that actually commands the delivery rate. If the app sends a wrong rate, or the displayed rate diverges from the delivered rate, the clinician's oversight is exactly what's been defeated.
- **"It just shows the current rate" is a safety-relevant function**, not a passive one. Displaying delivery rate is part of the control loop, and rule 3.3 covers it regardless.

## The prior question you should settle first

Is the app a **separate device** or a **component of the pump**? You said it's your own pump, which makes this live:

- **Component / part of the pump system** — same manufacturer, placed on the market as part of the pump with a single intended purpose and a single technical file. Then it isn't classified separately at all; it's covered by the pump's Class III certificate, and your job is to get it into that device's design controls, risk management, and NB scope.
- **Separate device in its own right** — placed on the market independently (e.g. app-store distribution, sold or CE-marked separately, own intended-purpose statement). Then it's its own Class III device with its own certificate, UDI, declaration of conformity, and clinical evaluation.

The fact that you have a standalone intended purpose statement written for it suggests you may be heading toward the second, which is materially more expensive. If it's genuinely inseparable from the pump, the first route is usually the right one — but that's a decision to make deliberately, not by drift.

## One thing worth verifying

Infusion pumps are commonly **Class IIb** under MDR (Rule 12, devices for administering medicinal products; escalated to III typically for implanted pumps, drug-device combinations, or closed-loop delivery of hazardous substances). Since the app's class is a direct copy of the pump's, confirm the pump's Class III basis is correct and documented — if the pump is actually IIb, so is the app.

## What Class III means downstream

- Annex IX conformity assessment: full QMS + technical documentation assessment by your notified body, **including design dossier examination** (Annex IX Chapter II) — not a route where the app can ride along unexamined.
- **IEC 62304 software safety Class C** — the app can contribute to death or serious injury via over-infusion, and there's no plausible external risk-control measure that segregates it down to B.
- Clinical evaluation under Article 61 with the higher Class III expectations, plus PMCF.
- IEC 62366-1 usability engineering is central here — rate-entry UI is where the real hazards live (decimal misplacement, unit confusion, unconfirmed changes).
- Because it's on a **general-purpose tablet**: you own the computing-platform requirements — OS version control, what happens on app crash / backgrounding / low battery / lost connectivity, and MDCG 2019-16 cybersecurity. A "fail safe" story for the pump when the controller disappears is mandatory.

Worth confirming the component-vs-separate-device call with your notified body before you build the technical file around either assumption — that choice is harder to reverse than the classification itself, and this analysis is engineering guidance, not a regulatory sign-off.

## Class III

Not IIa, not IIb. The reasoning most people get wrong here is that they reach for Rule 11 (the "software rule") and land on IIa. Rule 11 doesn't apply to your app.

**The rule that governs is an implementing rule, not a classification rule.** MDR Annex VIII, Chapter II, §3.3:

> *Software, which drives a device or influences the use of a device, shall fall within the same class as the device. If the software is independent of any other device, it shall be classified in its own right.*

Your app sets the delivery rate. That is the textbook definition of "drives a device." So it inherits the pump's class — III. You never get to the Annex VIII Chapter III rules at all.

**Why Rule 11 is a red herring:** Rule 11 covers software *intended to provide information which is used to take decisions*. It's about software that outputs information to a human who then acts. Yours acts on the pump directly. MDCG 2019-11 draws exactly this line — "software driving or influencing the use of a device" is a separate category from MDSW subject to Rule 11.

Two things in your framing that feel like they should lower the class but don't:

- **"It doesn't diagnose anything."** Irrelevant. Diagnosis is one route into Rule 11; therapy delivery is the other, and driving-software bypasses both.
- **"Under clinician control."** Also irrelevant. A clinician is in the loop on essentially every infusion pump, and the hazard — an entered or transmitted rate that isn't the intended rate — is not mitigated by the clinician being the one who typed it. If anything, the human-in-the-loop is the *source* of the classic overinfusion hazard (decimal point, wrong units, wrong concentration), which is why IEC 62366-1 usability engineering will be a focal point of your NB review.

The "shows the current rate" function is a monitoring/display claim that on its own might sit lower, but classification takes the highest applicable class across all the intended purposes, so it doesn't move anything.

## Two things to verify before you bank on this

**1. Is the pump genuinely Class III?** This matters because your app's class is entirely derivative. Under MDR Rule 12, a typical external infusion pump lands in **IIb** ("administering medicinal products... in a manner that is potentially hazardous"). Class III for a pump usually means one of: it's active *implantable* (Rule 8), it incorporates a medicinal substance (Rule 14), or it's indicated for a therapy that pulls it up. If someone carried "Class III" over from an old MDD determination or an FDA classification (FDA Class II/III uses a completely different scheme and doesn't map), re-derive it. If the pump turns out to be IIb, your app is IIb.

**2. Is the app a *part* of the pump, or a device placed on the market in its own right?** Same manufacturer, so you have a genuine choice, and it's the bigger commercial decision:

- **As a component of the pump system** — it lives inside the pump's technical documentation, shares its CE certificate, its UDI-DI, its declaration of conformity. One certificate to maintain. But every app release is a change to a Class III device, so it runs through your change-control and possibly notified body notification for significant changes. Ties your app release cadence to pump lifecycle governance.
- **As a standalone device / accessory** — its own classification (still III via §3.3), own UDI, own DoC, own clinical evaluation and PMCF. More overhead, but decouples release cycles and lets you support multiple pump models.

For a single-pump control app from the pump's own manufacturer, folding it into the pump's technical documentation is usually the cleaner path. If you're planning to support a family of pumps or third-party ones later, standalone is worth the overhead.

## What Class III actually costs you

- **Conformity assessment**: Annex IX (full QMS + technical documentation assessment, including design examination) or Annex X+XI. No self-certification, no Annex IX Chapter I-only shortcut.
- **Clinical evaluation** under Article 61, with a **PMCF plan mandatory** and a **PSUR annually** (not every two years).
- **IEC 62304 software safety class C** almost certainly — overinfusion can contribute to death. Note this is a *separate* determination from the MDR class; you can't infer one from the other, though here they both come out at the top. Class C means full architectural design, detailed design down to unit level, and unit verification documented.
- **Standards in scope**: IEC 62304, IEC 62366-1 (usability — do a proper summative validation on rate entry), ISO 14971, IEC 82304-1 (health software products), and IEC 60601-1 / 60601-1-8 / **60601-2-24** apply at the pump-plus-app system level even though your part is software. Plus MDCG 2019-16 for cybersecurity.
- **The tablet is uncontrolled hardware.** Your risk file needs to handle OS auto-updates, competing apps, touchscreen mis-registration, display failure or dimming, battery depletion mid-infusion, and loss of the pump link. Specify minimum hardware/OS in the IFU and state what happens to a running infusion when the app dies — the pump must fail safe independently.

Expect the notified body to want patient population, clinical setting, user profile, the drug classes/therapies in scope, and explicit exclusions.
